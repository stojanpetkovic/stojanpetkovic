On **September 24, 2026** Google announced a new spam update, its fourth of the year after March, June and August. Earlier ones finished in a day or two; this one took about two weeks and completed on **October 8, 2026**. It applies to all languages and all countries.

Google didn't say which kind of spam it was aimed at. So there's no single cause to hunt for. The sensible move is to check your site against the rules Google has published for years. Below is what we know, who these updates tend to hit, and the checklist I use on client sites.

## What a spam update is (and isn't)

Google runs two kinds of big changes. A **core update** changes how quality is judged across all pages. A **spam update** improves the systems that catch pages breaking [Google's spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies). If your site doesn't break those policies, a spam update shouldn't hit it directly. You might still move because sites around you dropped or climbed.

## What we know about this one

- It started on September 24 and finished on October 8, 2026.
- It's global and covers all languages.
- Google didn't name a specific spam type, and it wasn't a link-spam-only update.
- People tracking rankings saw stronger movement in several waves during the rollout, so some owners only noticed a drop days in.

Anything else you read on forums is guesswork. Trust your own Search Console data.

## How to check if you were hit

1. Open **Google Search Console** and go to the Performance report.
2. Compare two or three weeks before September 24 with the period after October 8.
3. Look at clicks and impressions, not only average position.
4. Split pages into groups: service pages, city pages, blog. If only one group fell, that's where the problem is.
5. Check **Security & Manual actions**. Anything there is a separate issue, handled with a reconsideration request.

If nothing dropped, don't change anything in a panic. Stable sites are the winners here.

## What to check on your site

These are the things Google's policies penalize most often, and the ones I see on local business sites:

- **Pages made at scale.** Hundreds of "service + city" pages with the same text and only the town name swapped. A city page only makes sense if it has something real about that place: jobs you did there, prices, timelines, photos.
- **Content written only for Google.** Piles of articles generated by a tool with no review and no experience behind them. AI isn't the problem by itself; low-value content in bulk is.
- **Bought or swapped links.** "100 backlinks for $50" packages, paid articles without disclosure, networks of sites linking to each other.
- **Someone else's content on your domain.** Third parties publishing off-topic articles on your site to borrow its reputation.
- **Sneaky redirects and hidden text.** Pages Google sees differently from visitors, text in the same color as the background, keyword stuffing.
- **Hacked sections.** Old WordPress plugins are a common way in for spam pages the owner doesn't even know about. Search `site:yourdomain.com` and look for pages you never made.

## If you were hit

1. Find the group of pages that dropped and be honest: would you show them to a customer?
2. Merge weak pages into one good page, or delete and redirect them.
3. Remove or disavow bad links you paid for.
4. Fix the technical side: hacked pages, duplicates, slow load times.
5. Be patient. Recovery from an algorithmic drop isn't instant. Google has to recrawl the site, and a clearer recovery often shows up only after the next major update.

## What this means for local service businesses

For a contractor, clinic or rental, the lesson is simple. Ten pages that truly describe your work beat two hundred that exist only for Google. Real photos, real examples, honest prices and reviews are hard to fake, which is exactly why they last.
